Denial Intelligence

Health insurers whose claim-denial behaviour is abnormal for their own market — and, where the data allows it, whether their members won when they appealed.

Scope

Findings

Why the comparison is within market

Dental insurers deny twice as often as medical ones, normally.

The first version of this ranked every insurer together and returned a list that was entirely dental plans. Dental is not scandalous; it is just different. Ranking across markets finds the market, not the outlier.

Why “above the median” is not enough

A rate measured over 40 appeals is not the same evidence as one measured over 1,730.

Every insurer below files a different number of appeals. Being a few points above the market’s overturn rate is strong evidence over thousands of appeals and meaningless over a few dozen. The curve is the line a rate has to clear to count as corroboration — it tightens as the sample grows.

Method

Four decisions, each of which changed the output.

  1. Compare inside a market

    Each insurer is scored against peers in the same market only. A market needs at least insurers before “unusual” means anything, which excludes SHOP entirely.

  2. Collapse issuer-wide figures before comparing

    Claim counts are reported per issuer, not per plan, and repeat on every row. Grouping the obvious way entered one insurer three times and compared its whole national book against a handful of peers. Each issuer is collapsed to one row per state and attributed to the market where it sells the most plans.

  3. Median and MAD, not mean and standard deviation

    Outliers inflate the standard deviation, which raises the very threshold meant to catch them. With six insurers planted at 45–58%, the modified z-score catches all six and the ordinary one catches none.

  4. Two thresholds, and a second signal that has to be real

    A modified z above is reportable on the denial rate alone. Above it is reportable only when the appeal record corroborates it — and corroboration is a one-sided significance test, not a larger number. On this data the test changed no findings; the narrowest cleared it by standard errors. That is the point: it is what keeps next year’s twelve-appeal fluke out.

What this does not show

One year of data, in-network claims only, and every figure is self-reported by the insurer to CMS — an insurer that under-reports denials looks better here. A high denial rate is not proof of wrongdoing: it can reflect a sicker population, a stricter benefit design, or accurate adjudication of bad claims. That is precisely why the appeal outcome is required before the strongest label is used, and why the other tier is named for what is actually known rather than what is implied.
